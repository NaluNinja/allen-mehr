# Perfect Competition Decision Memo

**To:** Farm manager  
**Purpose:** Recommend the profit-maximizing planting plan under current prices, labor conditions, and crop caps.

---

## Decision

The plan is to plant 10 beds of tomatoes, 20 beds of carrots, and 30 beds of mesclun. It uses 60 of the farm’s 64 beds and produces seasonal profit of approximately $42,761. Tomatoes stop at 10 beds because the marginal cost of bed 11 exceeds the tomato price. Carrots and mesclun stop at their production caps even though their marginal costs remain below price.

## Judgment call

If the farm can relax only one constraint, it should expand the carrot cap by one bed. The carrot-cap shadow price is $352.49, compared with $246.47 for mesclun, a difference of $106.02 per bed. Because four beds remain unused, the farm does not need more land; it needs capacity or permission to plant another carrot bed. This conclusion applies to a one-bed change and depends on current prices, labor costs, and caps.

The farm should continue growing carrots and mesclun in the mixed plan despite their negative standalone profits. Those standalone results include fixed and shared costs, whereas the short-run production decision depends on whether an additional bed covers its variable and marginal costs. Tomatoes differ because they are profitable on a standalone basis at the chosen output and stop because the next bed’s marginal cost exceeds price.

## What would change the answer

Tomatoes stop at 10 beds for prices between $8,248.59 and $9,390.72. Below $8,248.59, the 10th bed no longer pays; above $9,390.72, the 11th bed becomes profitable. This leaves approximately $551 of downside room and $591 of upside room before tomato output changes. A higher carrot or mesclun cap could also use some of the four unused beds.

## Stage 1 provenance and limits

Stage 1 predictions about which constraints would matter were reasoned from the model’s structure. In contrast, the 5,277-hour labor total was a published check figure used for validation, not an independently generated forecast. The core conclusion remains sound because it rests on the qualitative finding that labor was slack when planting stopped, not on that precise total.

Matching known check figures is confirmation rather than independent validation. The per-crop labor block in `Mix!B27:E28` was not independently tested, so its use to explain the unexamined carrot and mesclun marginal-cost patterns should be treated cautiously.
