# Perfect Competition — Engagement Brief

## Problem Statement

The garden must decide how many beds of tomatoes, carrots, and mesclun to plant for one 36-week season. It cannot control what customers pay, so its decision is limited to choosing the crop mix that makes the best use of its resources.

The farm has 64 beds, fixed seasonal costs, the farmer's 720 field hours, and the option to hire up to four temporary workers. Each crop also has a maximum number of beds. Because additional beds become more labor-intensive as planting increases, the goal is to choose the number of beds of each crop that earns more than it costs in added labor and supplies.

## Hypothesis

I think the farm will plant about 10 tomato beds, 20 carrot beds, and 30 mesclun beds. I expect carrots and mesclun to reach their bed limits because their labor needs increase more slowly as additional beds are planted. I expect tomatoes to stop around 10 beds because their labor needs rise much faster with each additional bed, so the next tomato bed may cost more to grow than its $8,800 selling price. This would leave four beds unused, not because the farm runs out of workers, but because the remaining available tomato beds are no longer worth planting.

## Which Constraint I Expect to Bind

The real question is what stops the farm from planting more. It is not land: I expect 60 of the 64 beds to be planted, so if another bed were still worth planting, the space for it would be there. It is not labor either: I expect about 5,277 of the 6,480 available hours to be used, so hours are still left over when planting stops. What stops the farm is economics. Tomatoes stop at around 10 beds because the cost of growing the next tomato bed passes its $8,800 price, and that is what leaves four beds idle.

Carrots and mesclun are the exception, and they stop for a different reason. Both need relatively little labor and land per bed and stay profitable across their whole range, so what stops them is their own bed caps of 20 and 30 rather than rising cost. If those caps were raised, I would expect to plant more of both.

## How I Would Know I Was Wrong

- **Tomatoes reach their 20-bed cap.** The labor penalty on additional tomato beds is milder than I assumed, and tomatoes stay profitable well past the point where I predicted they would stop.
- **Carrots or mesclun finish below their caps.** Rising labor cost, not the caps, is what stops those crops — which would contradict my claim that they stay profitable across their whole range.
- **All 64 beds are planted, or the labor hours are used up.** My central claim is that the farm stops with four beds idle and hours to spare. If the model fills every bed or exhausts the labor budget, then land or labor is what stops the farm after all, and my explanation is wrong.
