Paying for Reliability: A Conditional Medicare Premium for Generic Cisplatin and Carboplatin

The Challenge

In late 2022, FDA inspectors at an Intas Pharmaceuticals plant in Ahmedabad, India, found bags of torn and discarded manufacturing records, and an analyst destroying records by pouring acetic acid into a trash bin (U.S. Food and Drug Administration [FDA], 2023). An FDA import alert followed. That one facility supplied more than half of the U.S. market for generic cisplatin and carboplatin (KFF Health News, 2023), two decades-old, inexpensive chemotherapy drugs used against a wide range of cancers. When the plant's output stopped, no other supplier could make up the difference, and oncologists began rationing. One Virginia clinic cut carboplatin to 60% of the optimal dose, then raised it to 80% once a shipment arrived (KFF Health News, 2023). National shortages reached 323 active drugs in the first quarter of 2024, the highest count in 23 years of tracking (American Society of Health-System Pharmacists [ASHP], 2024).

This paper argues that the cisplatin shortage was the predictable result of a market that pays nothing for reliability. I defend an amended version of the Senate Finance Committee's May 2024 discussion draft: a Medicare add-on payment for multi-year generic-injectable contracts, tied to a verified, performing alternate manufacturer. Wosińska and Frank (2024) criticized the draft because paying providers for meeting contract standards does not guarantee a reliable supply. They proposed checking whether contracts actually deliver and paying on that. I agree with them. My amendment builds on their idea by making that check a condition of payment.

The Economics

Three course concepts explain why the shortage happened and why it lasted.

Fixed costs and barriers to entry. A sterile-injectable line is expensive and slow to build and qualify. Before any plant spending, bringing a generic injectable to market costs an estimated $5.9 million to $12.2 million (Holtkamp & Murphy, 2024, p. 2). Injectables also take longer to earn that back than orals: 25 months against 13 in the middle scenario, with a 36-month return on investment of 17% against 55% (Holtkamp & Murphy, 2024, pp. 4, 6). With high entry costs and thin returns, few firms hold spare capacity. Forty percent of generic markets have a single manufacturer (Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation [ASPE], 2025). As Figure 1 shows, once supply breaks, the median injectable shortage lasts 4.6 years, compared with 1.6 years for oral drugs (about 2.9 times as long), which reflects how slowly new sterile capacity can come online.

Price-inelastic demand. A patient's carboplatin dose depends on body size and the treatment protocol, not on price. Because demand barely moves with price, a supply cut can't clear through a higher price the way the textbook model predicts; it clears through rationing instead. The price signal that would normally attract new suppliers is muted, and it arrives far too late to help a patient in the middle of treatment.

Market structure and price pressure. Generic prices are held down by payment rules such as Maximum Allowable Cost lists. These set a ceiling on what a payer will pay "regardless of which manufacturer is chosen" (Rebitzer & Rebitzer, 2024), so a reliable supplier earns nothing more than an unreliable one. The average net price of generics fell by more than half between 2016 and 2022 (KFF Health News, 2023). Annual price declines ran 10–15% in 2017–2018 and eased to 5–10% by 2021 (Rebitzer & Rebitzer, 2024). At 36 months, about 70% of generic injectable entries are underwater on net profit (Holtkamp & Murphy, 2024, p. 7). Exits followed: Akorn closed, Lannett filed for Chapter 11, and Teva moved away from low-value generics (KFF Health News, 2023). In a market that rewards only the lowest price, no firm is paid to keep a backup source running.

Analysis: What Would a Premium Have to Pay?

I can't show that a premium creates plant capacity, but I can test whether it changes a manufacturer's decision to enter or stay in the market, using a breakeven on the cost of entry.

The cost of goods sold for generic injectables runs 42% of revenue, which leaves a 58% contribution margin (Holtkamp & Murphy, 2024, p. 2). A product covers its entry cost E when 0.58 × R ≥ E, where R is revenue over 36 months. If a price uplift u is treated as pure margin, the uplift needed to break even is:

u = E / R − 0.58

Under the medium scenario, a product with $15.8 million in 36-month revenue needs no uplift. At $10 million it needs about 34%, and at $5 million about 125%. The zero-uplift point is $10.2 million in the low scenario and $21.1 million in the high one. A flat premium large enough for a mid-sized product falls far short for small ones, and small, low-revenue products are the old, cheap generics most likely to run short. The cost of the premium therefore depends on how far down the revenue distribution it has to reach.

The estimate has four limits. First, it is a first-order bound: it doesn't discount within the 36-month window as the source does at 8.82%, and it holds volume and per-unit cost fixed. Second, it measures the cost of entry, not the cost of building a plant, so it can't compare the premium with loans for plant construction on cost of capital. Third, the underlying data cover generics launched between 2018 and 2021, not decades-old drugs like cisplatin. The authors note that older generics are "especially prone to shortage risk" and offer only "suggestive evidence linking profitability to shortage risk" (Holtkamp & Murphy, 2024, pp. 3, 9). Fourth, the formula assumes the manufacturer actually receives the uplift. Because the add-on is paid to hospitals and physicians, that is not guaranteed.

The recommendation holds only if the premium costs less than the shortages it prevents. Managing shortages cost U.S. hospitals an estimated $894 million in labor in 2023, or 20.2 million hours (Vizient, 2025). That is about double the 2019 estimate of $360 million (Vizient, 2019), and cisplatin and carboplatin are named among the highest-impact shortages. The figure is a floor, since it leaves out off-contract purchases, which ran about 214% above primary-distributor prices, and delays in patient care. Vizient also has a commercial stake in shortage management. Spread over 323 active shortages, the labor cost comes to about $2.8 million per shortage per year. An injectable shortage lasts a median 4.6 years, so one episode costs about $13 million in labor. In the breakeven formula, the dollar premium a product needs is E − 0.58R, so it can never be more than the entry cost, which is $9.2 million in the middle scenario and $12.2 million in the high one. Per product, then, the premium comes in under the cost of a shortage, but not by much. Medicare would also pay the premium on every enrolled product, not only the ones that would have run short. The premium pays off only if the premium per product, divided by the share of enrolled products that would otherwise go short, stays under about $13 million. At the full $9.2 million, more than 70% of enrolled products would have to be ones that would otherwise run short. For a product with $10 million in revenue, the premium is about $3.4 million, and the required share drops to about one in four. The margin is modest, and it depends on aiming the premium at the products most likely to run short. The test also can't show that the money reaches manufacturers. The conditions in the recommendation are meant to handle that.

Recommendation

Medicare should pay hospitals and physicians a reliability add-on for contracts of three years or more, at meaningful volume, with quality-vetted sterile-injectable manufacturers. The add-on should pay only when the contract names a qualified alternate manufacturer that is verified and actually supplying, with payment tied to auditable outcomes such as fill rate and FDA inspection record, and contracts re-bid at term end.

The Senate draft already requires contingency agreements with alternate manufacturers (Senate Committee on Finance, 2024), but nothing checks whether they work. Wosińska and Frank (2024) also warn that the program's compliance costs will limit how much of the payment passes through to manufacturers, and they propose paying on whether contracts actually deliver. My amendment makes that check a condition of payment. The provider gets paid only if a verified alternate supplier is actually shipping and the contract meets fill-rate and inspection standards. The money goes to the provider, but the entry decision belongs to the manufacturer, so the provider should collect only when a real manufacturer relationship is producing supply.

Why not the Hamilton Project package? Wosińska and Frank (2023) propose $2 billion in low-interest, partly forgivable loans for facility upgrades, a pay-for-performance program for buffer inventory, and a federal stockpile. Loans lower the cost of building a plant. But the breakeven shows that once a product is on the market, it keeps 58 cents of every sales dollar after production costs. The problem is that a small product doesn't sell enough to pay back its entry cost. A cheaper plant doesn't change how much a product sells. A multi-year volume commitment does, and it also gives a manufacturer something to borrow against when it adds capacity. I treat the loans as a possible complement, not a substitute. The same authors wrote the Hamilton proposal and the critique of the Senate draft, so they have a stake in this comparison. Their $2 billion is not budget-neutral either; if cost is an objection to my premium, it applies to their proposal as well.

The strongest objection. A premium could lock in incumbents and be administered by the same purchasing structures that produced the race to the bottom. It could protect margins without adding any capacity. The conditions are meant to address this. A premium that pays only when a second qualified source exists pays for redundancy, not incumbency. Re-bidding at term end keeps the protection temporary. Fill-rate and inspection conditions make the payment auditable. Paying nothing for reliability also has a cost; it just shows up later, as rationed chemotherapy doses rather than a budget line.

Funding. The draft lets the Secretary reduce or waive Medicaid inflation rebates for generics in shortage, and it requires participating hospitals to certify they took no discounts, 340B included, on those drugs (Senate Committee on Finance, 2024). Waiving the rebate raises 340B ceiling prices, so part of the cost shifts to 340B hospitals. The draft names no offset. My recommendation adds an explicit requirement that the Secretary keep Medicaid whole.

Conclusion

The cisplatin shortage came from a market with high entry costs, inelastic demand, and pricing that pays nothing for a second supplier. The breakeven shows that bringing manufacturers into these markets is affordable for mid-sized products and expensive for small ones. Set against the labor cost of a shortage, the premium comes out ahead only narrowly, and only if most of the money goes to products that would otherwise run short. That is why the add-on has to be targeted and conditional to work.
